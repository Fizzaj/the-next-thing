About Me

Founder photo

I help people work out what to do next.

A lot of my work starts with a problem that isn't quite as straightforward as it first appears. A business is introducing a new system, but the real challenge is getting people to work differently. A process isn't working particularly well, but nobody is quite sure where the problem sits. Someone has years of experience and knowledge, but hasn't worked out what they could do with it. Or there is a good idea somewhere in the business, but it hasn't yet become something practical. These are the sorts of problems I enjoy working on.

My background is in management consulting with much of my professional experience focused on technology implementation, including ERP and EPR programmes. I've worked across areas including business analysis, stakeholder engagement, change management, training and adoption, operational improvement, research, and business development.

That experience has given me a fairly simple way of looking at problems:

1. Understand what is there
2. Work out what isn't working
3. Look at what could be different
4. Then work out what is actually worth doing.

I don't think change is automatically a good thing. Sometimes the existing way of doing something is perfectly sensible; sometimes it isn't. The interesting part is working out which is which. Most people have more to work with than they realise — years of experience, questions people regularly ask them, content they've created, an audience they've built, knowledge they've picked up through their career. But those things don't necessarily arrive neatly packaged as an opportunity.

How I work

I utilise AI to supplement research, analyse information, spot patterns, explore ideas and make processes more efficient. But I don't think the interesting question is simply: What can AI create? It's: What is actually worth creating?

That still requires judgement, an understanding of people, and a knowledge of the problem you're trying to solve. It requires someone who can look at a collection of information and see the connections between things that might not be obvious at first. That's where I come in.

I'm used to asking questions, making sense of complex information, understanding different perspectives and turning ideas into something that can actually be used. My professional experience in research, sales and business development has given me a commercial perspective on understanding markets, customers and opportunities. I use this to help creators look at what they already know, understand what people need, and explore whether there is something useful they could create from it — that might become a guide, course, toolkit, template, membership, programme or something else entirely.

I'm less interested in starting with: What digital product should we make? I'd rather start with: What do you already have here, and is there something people would actually find useful?

The Next Thing: My Offering

This thinking sits behind The Next Thing. I'm interested in the space between where you are now and what could come next. Not trying to predict the future, not changing things simply because something newer exists, and not creating things just because we can. Instead, understanding what is already there, finding the useful possibilities, and working out what is worth doing next.

So what does this look like in practice?

  • Understanding your expertise and what makes it useful
  • Exploring what your audience or customers actually need
  • Finding potential product ideas
  • Turning knowledge into a clear product concept
  • Research, structure and develop ideas for increased engagement and revenue potential
  • Working out how a product could be positioned
  • Testing whether an idea is worth pursuing
  • Developing the product and thinking about how it could be launched

I don't always know the answer when we start, but I don't think that's a bad thing. I'd rather start with the right question, understand what's actually going on, and work it out from there. If you're trying to work out what comes next, let's talk.

My Philosophy

You'll see the Japanese symbol of Enso throughout my brand. I chose it because I like the idea of the circle as something that brings different things together, while the open form leaves room for movement, development and whatever comes next. It reminds me to look at the whole picture, to not assume everything needs to be perfect, to stay relaxed and stay open to what you might discover, and remember that the next thing doesn't have to be the final thing.
